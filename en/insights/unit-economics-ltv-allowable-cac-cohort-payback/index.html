Unit Economics for Media Buyers: LTV, Allowable CAC, and Cohort Payback

The LTV:CAC ratio done on contribution, allowable acquisition cost and first-order ROAS floors, payback in months, cohort curves that expose eroding quality, and pricing the second order.

Marketing is ultimately an investment, writes Azra Kassam at Shopify (January 26, 2026): you eventually expect a payoff, not a pile of receipts. The question every growing brand must answer is whether its marketing spend is building profit or burning cash, and the clearest instrument for that answer is the relationship between what a customer is worth and what that customer cost. But there is a second, quieter question that decides whether a growing brand survives its own growth, and nine.am (May 4, 2026) puts it bluntly: your CAC is not the problem; your payback period is. Return on ad spend tells you the return on a campaign. It does not tell you how long your money is tied up.

This article assembles the unit economics of the customer from six sources: Shopify's ratio guide (January 26, 2026), AdsX's payback ROAS framework (June 10, 2026), Drivepoint's cohort analysis practice (July 20, 2026), Andrew Lauchner's second-order pricing method from The Second Order (2026), nine.am's payback modeling (May 4, 2026), and The DTC Playbook's benchmark ranges (reviewed October 2026). Company figures and case numbers are cited as the sources' own accounts; benchmarks are guide ranges, not rules; our interpretation is marked.

The two numbers and their ratio

The LTV to CAC ratio compares what a single customer brings over their lifetime to what it cost to acquire them. Shopify's formulas are the standard starting point. Lifetime value, based on gross revenue as is standard for ecommerce: average purchase value times average purchase frequency times average customer lifespan, so a fifty-dollar average order, four purchases a year, and a three-year lifespan produce a six-hundred-dollar lifetime value. New businesses without history should assume conservatively from early patterns: if most repeat customers return within six months, start with a one-year lifespan and refine as data arrives. Acquisition cost: total sales and marketing expenses divided by the number of new customers acquired, so twenty thousand dollars of spend that wins one hundred new customers is a two-hundred-dollar acquisition cost. Divide the first by the second and you have the ratio.

What counts as good depends on business model, margin, and stage, but Shopify's orientation points: software businesses aim for at least three to one, ecommerce typically sits between three and four to one, and two to one or less means you are close to break-even once costs beyond acquisition are counted, because the ratio alone does not factor overhead or the cost of goods. Their worked caution: a customer worth three hundred dollars of lifetime revenue at fifty percent gross margin leaves one hundred and fifty; against a fifty-dollar acquisition cost that is a healthy three to one with room for everything else. The counterintuitive warning matters as much: a ratio can be too high. Ten to one usually signals underinvestment in growth, not discipline, unless you are deliberately prioritizing profit or building a waitlist. The ratio is a steering instrument, not a score to maximize.

From lifetime value to an allowable acquisition cost

AdsX (June 10, 2026) turns the ratio into a bidding instrument with four steps. First, twelve-month lifetime value on a contribution basis: average order value times annual purchase frequency times gross margin, so eighty-five dollars, 2.8 purchases, and fifty-eight percent margin yield about 138 dollars. Second, allowable acquisition cost: divide that lifetime value by your target ratio, giving about forty-six dollars at three to one. Third, the break-even first-order return: one divided by gross margin, so at fifty-eight percent margin a single order covers its own acquisition cost at 1.72 times return, and anything below relies on repeat purchases to recover. Fourth, the payback-adjusted acquisition return target: acquisition cost divided by first-order revenue times margin, which in their worked example comes out at 0.93, meaning the brand can acquire customers at a first-order return below one, technically unprofitable on order one, and still hit its three-to-one target if customers return at the projected frequency.

Their fully worked supplement brand makes the mechanism concrete: seventy-two-dollar average order, sixty-two percent margin, 3.4 annual frequency, twelve-month lifetime value of about 152 dollars, allowable acquisition cost of about fifty-one dollars at three to one, and a payback-adjusted acquisition return target of 1.14, well below the two-times floor most supplement brands inherit. The payback timeline check is the discipline: order one barely breaks even after acquisition cost, order two arrives around day forty-five fully incremental, and by day ninety cumulative gross profit has recovered the acquisition cost nearly twice. They attach two fences. Payback windows vary by category, from forty-five to ninety days for beauty and skincare to one hundred and eighty to three hundred and sixty-five for high-priced electronics, and their table of category ranges is their own benchmark. And single-purchase categories like furniture should not use the framework at all: with no meaningful repeat recovery, the first order must carry the full economics. Finally, their warning on blended return: when returning customers make up thirty to fifty percent of revenue, blended return is systematically inflated, and a brand showing 3.5 blended may be acquiring at only 1.6. Acquisition economics must be read on new customers only.

Payback: the cash-flow lens

nine.am (May 4, 2026) argues payback sometimes matters more than return on ad spend for sustainable growth, because a brand with a five-month payback recycles cash into new campaigns while a brand with an eighteen-month payback is fronting the cost of every new customer for a year and a half. Their formula for direct-to-consumer: new-customer acquisition cost divided by contribution margin per order times monthly purchase frequency, with the warning that gross revenue or blended acquisition cost will distort the result. Their orientation: under six months is ideal for most categories; over twelve creates cash pressure that limits how fast you can scale. They also note why ecommerce cannot copy the software model: customers do not generate fixed monthly revenue, and one cohort may return within thirty days while another never buys again, which is why payback must be modeled by cohort rather than assumed as a clean curve.

Lauchner's invented brand in The Second Order shows the cash arithmetic at its starkest: a fifty-dollar order at sixty percent product margin with eight dollars of shipping, fulfilment, and fees contributes twenty-two dollars; against a forty-dollar acquisition cost every new customer arrives eighteen dollars underwater, and the business is paid on the second order, not the first. His payback-month method: for each acquisition cohort, cumulate contribution from every order to date, divide by customers acquired, and the payback month is the first month the running total reaches what you paid per customer. In the invented brand, each customer needs about 0.82 repeat orders to pay back, and if cohorts average 0.4 repeat orders by month six and 0.8 by month thirteen, payback lands in month fourteen. Then the management question: if your cash plan tolerates twelve months and payback lands in fourteen, you close the gap with cheaper acquisition or with second orders that arrive sooner and more often. The month tells you what the business can carry.

Cohorts: why averages lie

Drivepoint (July 20, 2026) supplies the measurement discipline. A cohort groups customers by a shared starting point, usually the month of their first order, and tracks each group forward: how many come back, what they spend, what they cost to serve. Segmentation groups customers by who they are and gives a snapshot; cohorts group by when they started and give a movie. The movie matters because averages lie. Their illustration: a blended repeat rate holding at thirty-two percent all year can hide customers acquired two years ago repeating at forty-one percent while the last six months repeat at twenty-three, the loyal early base padding the average while customer quality erodes underneath, invisible on any topline dashboard. The market context they cite: the average direct-to-consumer brand sees only 28.2 percent of first-time buyers return for a second purchase, acquisition costs climbed forty to sixty percent between 2023 and 2025, and Harvard Business Review's long-standing estimate puts acquiring a new customer at five to twenty-five times the cost of keeping one.

Their cohort types that map to money: acquisition cohorts by first-order month as the foundation; channel cohorts, because different channels recruit very different customers and channel-level retention reveals which acquisition cost is actually sustainable; product cohorts by first item purchased, because some items recruit one-and-done buyers while others reliably start a habit; and subscription versus one-time, whose curves must never be blended. Their case accounts, cited as such: VKTRY found most repeat buyers made their second purchase within thirty days of the first, moved email flows and direct mail earlier, and lifted thirty-day retention by ten percent; Earth Breeze modeled retention per segment across its channels and gained the confidence to set hard acquisition-cost guardrails while scaling. And the two honesty rules: use the full cost stack, because a cohort can look strong on revenue and lose money once returns and fulfilment are counted, so net-revenue lifetime value against fully loaded acquisition cost is the number a board will trust; and translate lifetime value into acquisition-cost tolerance, the answer to how much you can safely spend before you are out of money.

Pricing the second order

Lauchner's step-up method prices the most important conversion in retention from a plain order export. Pull average lifetime revenue for one-order customers and for customers with exactly two orders; the gap is the step-up, what one more order is worth per customer. His skincare file: two-order customers average 155 dollars of lifetime revenue against 66 for one-order customers, a step-up of 89 dollars, making a two-order customer worth 2.35 times a one-order customer. Two disciplines make the number survive a finance review: measure against exactly two orders, never two or more, because the heavy buyers in the two-or-more average inflate the gap; and treat the step-up as an upper bound for win-back offers, because it compares customers who returned on their own with those who did not. Multiply the step-up by the count of one-time buyers and you have the ceiling if every one of them ordered once more, in his file 13,526 buyers times 89 dollars, about 1.2 million. In consumables the step-up is often smaller than the first order, a ninety-dollar kit followed by a forty-dollar refill, which means the cost of winning the reorder must sit well under forty dollars and profit arrives on the third and fourth orders, so timing the reorder matters more than discounting it. Cut by entry product, the same math becomes what he calls cost per returner: which first products earn back their acquisition cost and which lean on the second order.

Benchmarks as guide rails, not verdicts

The DTC Playbook (reviewed October 2026) publishes the ranges an acquirer would model, drawn from Rob Ward's experience taking Quad Lock from zero to a five-hundred-million-dollar exit and cross-checked against widely cited data, and they insist the ranges are guides for where to look, not pass-or-fail marks. Gross margin sixty to eighty percent sets the ceiling on what you can spend to acquire, with beauty and supplements higher and apparel and electronics lower, and below about fifty percent growth usually becomes very hard to fund. Payback: under three months excellent, three to six the healthy standard, six to twelve workable but financing customers' purchases, over twelve fragile. The ratio: three to five to one measured on twelve-month contribution lifetime value, with two to three workable only when payback sits inside six months, below two red unless first orders are profitable, and above five usually meaning you are underinvesting in growth. Their first-order test deserves emphasis: contribution-neutral or positive after acquisition, because if the first order loses money, every new customer worsens cash. Judge yourself against your own trend as well as the range.

What this means for your operation

A working unit-economics system emerges from these sources. Compute lifetime value on contribution, not revenue, and derive allowable acquisition cost from a target ratio chosen against your payback window, not inherited from a benchmark. Know your break-even first-order return as one over margin, and set your acquisition return target from the payback-adjusted formula, so you know exactly how far below first-order break-even you can deliberately go. Model payback by acquisition cohort and by channel, in months of cumulative contribution, and compare it against what your cash plan can carry. Price the second order with the step-up so retention work has a budget justified in dollars. And read everything on new customers only, because blended numbers hide erosion for quarters.
