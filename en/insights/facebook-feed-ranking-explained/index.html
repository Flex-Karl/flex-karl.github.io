How Facebook decides what your customers see

Facebook's Feed ranking system holds over 100 prediction models that decide the order of what each person sees. Here is how it actually works, what pushes business posts down, and what to do about it.

What happens before your post appears

When someone opens Facebook, they never see everything their friends, followed Pages and Groups posted; there is too much of it. Instead, an AI system picks roughly 500 candidate posts for that person, scores each one, and shows them in order of predicted value. Meta describes four steps: gather the inventory, read thousands of signals, run prediction models, then rank by relevance score.

Two things sit outside this ranking: ads, and recommended content from accounts the person does not follow. Both are inserted after the connected posts are ordered. So when your Page publishes something, it competes inside this system for a slot in front of each individual follower - person by person, not once for everyone.

The four steps, in plain terms

Step 1: Gathering the inventory

First, the system collects every recent post the person could see: posts from friends, from Pages they follow and from Groups they joined, excluding content flagged for violating the Community Standards. Meta says it personalizes Feed for more than 2 billion users precisely because this pool is bigger than anyone could browse in one session. Ranking exists because attention is scarce, not because Meta wants to hide your post.

Step 2: Reading thousands of signals

Next, the system reads thousands of signals about each candidate: who created it, whether it is a photo, video, live or link, how the viewer treated similar posts in the past, their history with the Page, and technical context like device and language. Meta publishes the category list openly. The striking thing about that list is how little of it is about the words you write: it is dominated by records of real behavior, yours and your audience's.

Step 3: Making predictions

More than 100 prediction models then turn signals into forecasts about this specific person: how likely they are to comment, to share, to keep watching the video, to visit the Page afterward, to hide the post or to report it. Meta groups these into predictions about actions the person will take, about how they will spend time, about their interest in whoever shared the post, and about how others will respond if they engage. Not every model runs on every post; Meta notes that some predictions apply only when a post is relevant to them. Some models work against you from the start: predictions that a person will hide, snooze or report a post exist specifically to reduce its distribution.

The models are not guessing alone. Meta also runs surveys asking people whether a post was worth their time, and those answers feed predictions about what other people will find worthwhile. And because ranking is personalized, the same prediction carries different weight for different people: for someone who never taps Like, time spent reading may say more than reactions ever could.

Step 4: Ranking by relevance score

Finally, the predictions merge into a relevance score for each of the roughly 500 posts, and Feed is ordered by that score, with a balancing pass so the person does not see the same format several times in a row. Only after this ordering are recommended posts and ads slotted in. Your post's position is therefore not a verdict on its quality in general; it is a per-person forecast made in the moment that person opens the app.

The signals a business Page can influence

Meta publishes the categories of signals its ranking reads. Most describe the viewer, which you cannot control. Three groups describe things you genuinely work on.

Signals about the post itself

  • The format - photo, video, live or link - matched against how this person usually treats that format.
  • How original the post is, measured as the percentage of content identical to other posts.
  • For links, how your domain's popularity on Facebook compares with the standing it has earned across the wider internet.
  • Whether the post's content has been rated false or partly false by Meta's independent fact-checking partners.
  • Whether the post is likely to violate the Community Standards.

Signals about your Page

  • How many times people have viewed your Page, and how many follow it, over time.
  • The total comments, reactions and shares your Page's content has earned.
  • How many confirmed Community Standards violations the account has accrued.

Signals about the viewer

The largest category is the viewer's own history: how often they interact with you, what formats they prefer, how they split attention between friends, Pages and Groups. You cannot edit these signals, but they explain something every Page admin notices: the same post performs very differently across followers, because it is re-ranked for each of them.

What quietly pushes posts down

Meta also publishes what reduces distribution. These are not rumors; they are listed on its Transparency Center:

  • Headlines that withhold information to lure a click.
  • Posts that draw a high volume of user reports.
  • Content rated false or partly false by independent fact-checking partners.
  • Posts the system predicts a person will hide, snooze or report.
  • Posts predicted to draw angry reactions.

One demotion signal deserves a closer look, because it catches legitimate businesses by surprise. Meta maps the internet as a graph where domains with many inbound and outbound links sit at the center, and it flags domains whose Facebook clicks are disproportionate to that position. A new website that exists only to be promoted on Facebook can trigger exactly this pattern. Earning references beyond Facebook is not just good SEO; it is a documented input to how your linked posts are treated inside Facebook.

Integrity screening also happens earlier than most people assume: problematic content is down-ranked while candidates are selected, before the final scoring even runs.

Recommended content: a second door into Feed

Alongside connected ranking, a separate AI system picks public posts from accounts the person does not follow and inserts them as recommendations. It runs the same four-step shape - inventory, signals, predictions, ranking - over a slice of public content, excluding anything that violates the Community Standards or Meta's Recommendation Guidelines. People can tune it with Show more and Show less, which temporarily raise or lower the score of that post and others like it.

For a business Page this matters because it is the one organic route to people who never followed you. Our reading: recommendation ranking leans on the same family of engagement and time-spent predictions, so content that genuinely holds attention with your existing audience is also the content most likely to travel beyond it.

Your audience has more control than you think

People can add your Page to Favorites, which prioritizes it in their Feed; snooze it for 30 days; unfollow it; hide individual posts, which also teaches the system to show less similar content; or mark posts Interested or Not Interested, which directly raises or lowers their scores. There is also a Following tab that shows posts from followed accounts in plain chronological order, and a Reconnect option that restores accounts they unfollowed.

Reach is therefore not earned from the algorithm alone. It is granted, post by post, by people who can mute you in two taps - and Meta keeps adding controls that make their choices louder than your publishing schedule.

A worked example

To make this concrete, imagine a restaurant Page with 10,000 followers publishing two posts in one week. This scenario is an illustration, not a client story.

Post one is a flyer image announcing a new menu. The prediction models have little to work with: followers who usually scroll past image flyers, a format this audience rarely comments on, and no conversation in the first hours. Predicted actions are low, predicted hides are not, and the post lands mid-Feed for a fraction of followers.

Post two is a 40-second video of the chef preparing the new dish, captioned with a genuine question about which version viewers would order. The viewer cohort includes people who regularly watch short food videos to the end, and the early comments trigger the back-and-forth discussion predictions. Predicted watch time, comments and shares are all higher, so the post is ordered higher - for those specific people. Both posts came from the same Page in the same week. The algorithm did not change; the predicted behavior did.

Common mistakes that cost reach

Working from Meta's published demotion categories, these are the patterns we see Pages repeat:

  • Headlines that withhold the point to force a click - a documented demotion signal, not a style choice.
  • Asking directly for likes, shares and tags, which trains the system on empty engagement and regular readers learn to scroll past.
  • Reposting identical content, which the originality percentage measures literally.
  • Linking only to a website that has no standing outside Facebook, which trips the domain disproportionality signal.
  • Provoking arguments for engagement, when predicted angry reactions reduce distribution.
  • Ignoring the comment thread, abandoning the back-and-forth discussion the ranking explicitly predicts and rewards.

What this means for your content plan

The practical reading, from our work managing Pages: posts that start real conversations outperform announcements. Video that holds attention beats video that merely plays. Engagement bait and clickbait are scored against you. And a website that earns references beyond Facebook makes your linked posts easier to distribute. None of this requires gaming the system; the documented system already rewards it.

  • Brief every post against one question: what will a specific follower do with this - comment, share, watch, or scroll past?
  • Publish in the format your audience already lingers on, not the format that is fastest to produce.
  • Write headlines that say what the post contains; curiosity gaps are a documented demotion signal.
  • Answer comments early, because back-and-forth discussion is one of the explicitly predicted behaviors.
  • Build your website's standing beyond Facebook so your linked posts carry authority with them.

Frequently asked questions

Does paying for ads improve my organic reach?

Meta's documentation describes ads as a separate system inserted after organic ranking, not an input to it. The Feed system card says plainly that suggested content and advertisements are not powered by the ranking AI it describes. Nothing in the published signal or prediction lists connects ad spend to organic position. Treat the two as parallel channels: ads buy placement, ranking is earned per post per person.

Can my followers see posts in plain chronological order?

Yes. The Following tab shows followed accounts in reverse chronological order, and posts from Pages a person adds to Favorites are prioritized in their main Feed. Part of your audience may be reading you outside the ranked Feed entirely.

How do I know if a specific post was demoted?

There is no per-post notice. Meta publishes the categories of what gets reduced - clickbait, reported content, fact-check ratings, predicted hides and angry reactions - and those categories are the checklist. When a post underperforms, audit it against that list before blaming timing.

Is any of this final?

No. Meta states that signals and models change frequently as the system learns, and its system cards carry update dates. Treat this article as a dated snapshot of the published mechanics - we update it when Meta does.
