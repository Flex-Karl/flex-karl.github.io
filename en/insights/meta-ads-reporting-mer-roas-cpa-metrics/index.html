Meta Ads Reporting: MER, ROAS, CPA and the Metrics That Actually Decide

Why platform ROAS overstates, how MER anchors reporting to real revenue, which metrics trigger action and which are noise, the daily weekly monthly cadence, and the 2026 platform changes every report should footnote.

Ads Manager offers hundreds of metrics, and that is the problem. More data does not mean better decisions; it usually means slower decisions and misplaced attention. Brands spend hours analyzing CPM trends when the issue is creative fatigue, and celebrate a 6x retargeting ROAS when the retargeting campaign is cannibalizing conversions that would have happened anyway. The 27Five reporting guide (March 19, 2026) sorts every metric into three buckets that this guide adopts: decision metrics that trigger action, health metrics that signal emerging problems, and vanity metrics that look important but rarely change what you do. Its onboarding finding is diagnostic: ask brands which five metrics they check most, and the usual answers are ROAS, CPC, CPM, impressions and reach, of which only one, ROAS properly contextualized, connects to a business outcome. This guide builds the reporting layer from the ground up: why platform ROAS overstates, the blended metric that anchors reality, the acquisition number Meta cannot report for you, the health metrics that warn early, the cadence that turns numbers into decisions, and the 2026 platform changes every report should footnote. Five sources from March to November 2026 ground the claims.

Why platform ROAS overstates

Ads Manager is an excellent interface and an unreliable narrator, in AdsRunner's words (June 30, 2026): built to show Meta's contribution in the most favorable defensible light. This is not an accusation of fraud; it is how attribution works when the platform grading the exam also wrote it. The AdChat guide (November 2, 2026) enumerates the five mechanisms. Attribution windows: by default Meta claims a purchase if the buyer clicked an ad in the last seven days or viewed one in the last day, so a customer who saw your ad, searched your brand, clicked a Google result and bought is counted by Meta, and possibly by Google too. Modelled conversions: since the iOS privacy changes, part of the number is Meta's statistical estimate of conversions it could not observe. Refunds and cancellations never come back out: a $200 purchase refunded next week is still $200 of conversion value in the report. Existing customers: retargeting shown to people who were going to buy anyway gets full credit, and Meta cannot tell new from repeat customers unless you set that up. Delayed reporting: the ROAS you see today for last week keeps changing for up to 28 days as late conversions land, so the number you decided on is not the number that ends up in the report. AdsRunner adds the structural point: blended campaigns take cheap existing demand first, so reported ROAS reflects harvested demand as much as created demand, and platform ROAS never asks the only question incrementality cares about, what would have happened without the ad. AdChat's worked example makes it visceral: Meta's ROAS saying 5.2 on $3,000 of spend means $15,600 of attributed value in a month where total real revenue was $12,000.

MER: the anchor that attributes nothing

The antidote is a blended metric. MER, the marketing efficiency ratio, is total revenue divided by total ad spend for the same period across all channels. Shopify's explainer (July 18, 2026) gives the clean example: $200,000 in revenue over $50,000 in marketing spend is a MER of 4, four dollars earned per dollar spent, and it notes Gartner's figure that marketing budgets ran at 7.8 percent of company revenue in 2026, nearly flat from 7.7 percent in 2025, which is exactly the planning conversation MER informs. The AdChat version is smaller and just as clear: $12,000 of shop revenue over $4,000 of total ad spend is a MER of 3.0. The reason MER works is that it attributes nothing: organic, email and word-of-mouth revenue sit in the numerator alongside everything else, so the question it answers is whether the money spent on ads grew the business, not whether Meta's model thinks it did. It cannot be gamed by a longer attribution window, and it drops when ads stop working even if platform ROAS stays flat, because customers Meta would have claimed anyway stop showing up in the real total. AdsRunner's operating habit is the reconciliation: each period, put Meta-reported revenue next to actual back-end revenue and total spend, and treat the gap as the most important number in your Meta reporting, the one Ads Manager will never show you. When Ads Manager claims a 6x ROAS and your MER has not improved, the platform is claiming credit it did not earn. 27Five's action triggers complete the loop: MER improving as you raise spend means the new spend is incremental, keep scaling; MER declining as you raise spend means diminishing returns, optimize first; MER stable while budget shifts between channels means the channels are substituting, not adding, so investigate incrementality.

Break-even ROAS: the number your margin sets

A MER of 3.0 sounds fine until costs enter. AdChat's arithmetic is the version worth taping next to the dashboard. First, gross margin after product costs and fees: revenue minus cost of goods, payment fees, shipping, packaging and returns; say 55 percent. Second, break-even ROAS is one divided by the margin: at 55 percent, that is 1.82, and any spend returning less than 1.82 times its cost in revenue loses money. Third, profit after ads is revenue minus refunds, minus product costs, minus ad spend, the number that actually matters. On the example shop, $12,000 of revenue minus $5,400 of costs minus $4,000 of ad spend leaves $2,600 of profit after ads. This is why benchmarks and rules of thumb can never set your thresholds: a brand at 70 percent margin breaks even at 1.43, a brand at 40 percent needs 2.5. The GoodMorning template guide (July 10, 2026, updated October 29) makes the same point about CPA: a cost per acquisition means nothing in isolation, only against a CAC ceiling derived from contribution margin and payback period, so put that ceiling in the report next to actual CPA and every campaign reads as under or over the line.

nCAC: the acquisition metric Meta cannot give you

27Five names nCAC, new customer acquisition cost, the single most important metric for ecommerce growth on Meta: total prospecting spend divided by new customers acquired, where new customer is defined from your first-party data, first purchase in your store system, because Meta does not reliably distinguish new from returning customers in its attribution. Unlike blended ROAS, nCAC cannot be inflated by repeat purchases or retargeting self-attribution, and it connects directly to unit economics: what you pay to acquire a customer against what a customer is worth. Its action triggers turn the metric into an operating rule: nCAC below target for seven or more days is a green light to scale budget; nCAC ten to twenty percent above target says diagnose, creative fatigue or audience saturation; nCAC thirty percent or more above target for five days says cut budget ten to twenty percent and diagnose. The target itself comes from your average order value, gross margin and payback period, not from any industry benchmark. The discipline underneath is what matters: an acquisition number built on your own customer records, so the platform's generosity with credit cannot leak into the metric you scale on.

Health metrics: the early-warning layer

Between the decision metrics and the noise sits a small set of health metrics worth monitoring weekly. Hook rate, three-second video views over impressions, is the leading indicator of creative health in 27Five's account: 30 percent or better for top-of-funnel prospecting, below 25 percent means the creative is losing attention, and a declining hook rate precedes CPA increases by three to five days, which is the window to queue replacements. Frequency is its most underutilized counterpart: below 2.0 for scaling campaigns and below 3.0 for retargeting as working targets, frequency above 2.5 with rising CPA confirms fatigue, and 27Five's finding is that frequency alerts catch fatigue-driven declines three to five days before CPA alerts do, because rising frequency means Meta is running out of new people to show the ad to. Around those two, the context metrics: CTR (a declining CTR alongside a stable hook rate means the hook works but the body does not close, with benchmarks of one to two percent for prospecting and two to four for retargeting), CPM (mostly outside your control, rising with competition and seasonality, Q4 up thirty to fifty percent platform-wide in their account), cost per add-to-cart (stable ATC cost with rising purchase cost points at checkout friction, not ad quality), conversion rate by landing page, and spend distribution, because if two or three ads consume seventy percent or more of an automated campaign's budget, the rest are not getting enough data to judge.

The vanity layer to demote

Some metrics look like the scoreboard and are not. Reach and impressions describe activity, not outcomes; you cannot optimize reach directly, the algorithm manages it. CPC is a function of CPM and CTR, both algorithm-managed, and a low CPC with no conversions is worse than a high CPC with strong conversion rates. Engagement, likes, comments, shares, does not correlate reliably with conversions: an ad with 500 likes and zero purchases performs worse than an ad with three likes and twenty purchases. And platform-reported ROAS itself belongs in the report as a secondary line, not the headline, for every reason in the first section. GoodMorning's list of monthly report mistakes starts with exactly that: leading with platform ROAS, the most flattering and least reliable number in 2026, and filling the top with vanity metrics that belong in an appendix if anywhere. The principle underneath: every metric that survives into a decision document should connect to an action you can take. If moving the number would not change what you do, it is decoration.

The cadence: daily, weekly, monthly

Reporting works as a rhythm, not a dashboard. 27Five's cadence: daily checks are for emergencies only, broken tracking and budget spikes; weekly is creative health and nCAC trends; monthly is MER and strategic direction. GoodMorning's monthly template gives the monthly layer its structure, and its rule is that every section produces exactly one number or one call, and the report ends in an action list, because a report that ends in a dashboard leaves the analysis for someone to skip on a busy month. The sections: a headline scorecard carrying blended MER for the month, driving the scale-hold-pull-back call; month-over-month deltas on spend, revenue and MER, because a single-month ROAS of 1.9 is a fact while a three-month line of 2.4 to 2.1 to 1.9 is a warning; spend efficiency, CPA against the CAC ceiling per campaign; the creative portfolio, share of spend on ads older than 30 days, the leading indicator that next month's efficiency is about to slip; the prospecting versus retargeting split with returns for each; reporting integrity, the gap between platform-reported and true figures; and the closing action list, act today, this week, monitor. The time argument supports the discipline: the Measured and Sequent Partners survey GoodMorning cites (2022) found 64 percent of DTC marketers spend more than nine hours a week on reporting, some upward of 25, so a monthly report should compress the month into decisions, not add another day to the pile.

Footnote the 2026 distortions

A 2026 report is only right if it accounts for what changed in the platform, and GoodMorning's integrity list is the current version. The January 2026 attribution window change lowered reported conversions for many accounts, breaking naive month-over-month math. The June 2026 metric deprecations make reach read lower than before. The July 1, 2026 European location fees hide two to five percent of cost outside Ads Manager, so reconcile to the invoice if you sell into Europe. The July 2026 removal of the off-Meta activity opt-out grew retargeting audiences on its own, which can lift retargeting numbers without any improvement in your work. And the October 28 product-browsing default changed what existing ads can show. Any month-over-month comparison crossing one of these dates needs a footnote, or the trend line lies. For context benchmarks, GoodMorning cites Triple Whale's analysis of nearly 35,000 ecommerce brands for full-year 2025: median Meta ROAS 1.86, CTR 2.19 percent, CPM $14.19 up roughly 20 percent year over year, CPA $38.19, conversion rate 1.6 percent. Use them as a coarse yardstick for whether you are in a normal band, never as a target; your margin structure sets your real thresholds.

A reporting checklist

How we report at NOVA.innovation

Everything from here is our practice, not platform documentation. In our paid media campaign management work, the client report leads with the blended number: MER computed from the client's actual revenue, with Meta-reported revenue next to it and the reconciliation gap stated in the same table. Acquisition efficiency is reported as nCAC built from the client's customer records, never Meta's new-versus-returning split, with the scale, diagnose and cut triggers agreed in advance so the monthly conversation is about decisions, not definitions. The break-even ROAS from the client's gross margin sits in the header of every report, so no CPA or ROAS is ever read without its floor. Weekly reviews carry the early-warning layer, hook rate, frequency, spend concentration, and the monthly report closes with the three-part action list, act today, this week, monitor. Every methodology break in the platform gets a dated footnote in the month it lands, because a trend line that crosses a silent measurement change is a story the data never told. And we say so in the report when the honest answer is that the numbers cannot yet separate what ads caused from what would have happened anyway.

The reporting layer in one paragraph: let Ads Manager be the place you optimize, never the place that grades its own homework. Anchor on MER from real revenue, scale on nCAC from your own records, judge every number against the break-even line your margin sets, watch the two early-warning metrics, keep the daily-weekly-monthly rhythm, and footnote every silent change in what the numbers mean. The gap between what the platform claims and what the business earned is the most important number in the report, and it only exists if you compute it.
