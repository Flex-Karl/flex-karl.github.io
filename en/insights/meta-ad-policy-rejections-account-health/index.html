Meta Ad Policy, Rejections, and Account Health: A Working Guide

How review really works, the personal-attributes trap, prohibited and restricted categories, the July 2026 health rewrite, and the discipline that recovers a restricted account.

Your ad just got rejected, and Meta's explanation is a generic policy link that tells you nothing useful about what you actually did wrong. Chris Pollard at Adsuploader (September 30, 2026) opens with that scene because it happens constantly: Meta's ad guidelines are scattered across dozens of pages in the Transparency Center, the Business Help Center, and the Community Standards, thorough but nearly impossible to use as a working reference when you are building ads and need a fast answer on whether something is allowed. This article distills the policy landscape, the rejection triggers that actually catch advertisers, and the account-recovery discipline, from five sources.

The sources: Adsuploader's practical guide to the Advertising Standards (September 30, 2026), ADS INFRA's compliant-copy guide to the personal attributes policy (November 14, 2026), AdNestly's appeal playbook for disabled ad accounts (November 3, 2026), Maxme's account recovery guide written for Ghanaian advertisers (September 25, 2026), and Meta's own introduction to the Advertising Standards in the Transparency Center. Platform policies change frequently; everything here is stated as of the sources' dates, and Meta's enforcement figures are labeled as Meta's own reports. Our interpretation is marked.

How review actually works

Every ad submitted to Meta is reviewed against the Advertising Standards before it can deliver, and Meta's own introduction states that advertisers running ads across its technologies must follow both the Community Standards and the Advertising Standards. Its four stated principles predict where enforcement bites hardest: protecting people from unsafe and discriminatory practices, protecting people from fraud or scams, promoting positive user experiences, and promoting transparency. Adsuploader adds the operational facts most advertisers miss. Review is primarily automated and typically completes within twenty-four hours, though restricted categories take longer. Approval is not permanent: Meta's documentation states that ads remain subject to review and re-review at all times and may be rejected or restricted at any time. And initial review may be partial, so an ad can run for days before a secondary review catches a violation. The compounding point is the one to internalize: a single rejection is an edit; repeated violations compound into account-level restrictions that are far harder to resolve than any single ad.

The personal-attributes trap

The policy that rejects more copy than almost anything else is also the most misunderstood. ADS INFRA quotes Meta's own words: ads must not contain content that asserts or implies personal attributes, including direct or indirect assertions or implications about a person's race, ethnicity, religion, beliefs, age, sexual orientation or practices, gender identity, disability, physical or mental health including medical conditions, vulnerable financial status, voting status, trade union membership, criminal record, or name. Two verbs do the heavy lifting: asserts, stating it outright, and implies, hinting at it. Because the policy covers indirect assertions, softening are you in debt into a nudge about the viewer's bills does not create compliance; it only changes which sentence the reviewer flags. Meta's stated rationale is user experience: ads that make assumptions about people could be perceived as intrusive, unsettling, or inaccurate, so advertisers are told to focus on the benefits of the product instead of the viewer.

The grammatical pattern behind most rejections, per both ADS INFRA and Adsuploader, is second-person language attached to a protected attribute: the word you plus a condition. The fixes are consistent across both sources, shifting from you have to people who want. Not allowed: are you struggling with diabetes; allowed: resources for people managing diabetes. Not allowed: tired of your acne; allowed: a skincare routine for clearer-looking skin. Not allowed: meet other Buddhists; allowed, in Meta's own published examples, meet Hindu singles online now. Meta explicitly permits broad references to attributes not on the prohibited list, such as calling someone a New Yorker, passing references to age groups, celebrity names, and you-or-your language with no personal attribute attached. The gut check both sources converge on: read the headline back and ask whether it accuses the reader of a condition; if a stranger would feel how do they know that about me, rewrite it. Keep the condition in the third person and the verb about wanting, and the ad clears review.

Prohibited, restricted, and special categories

Adsuploader's map of the Standards, current as of their September 30, 2026 check. Prohibited content is the hard-no list: illegal products, discriminatory practices in creative or targeting, dangerous organizations, misinformation debunked by fact-checkers, vaccine discouragement, hateful conduct, exploitation. The biggest enforcement engine is the consolidated Prohibited Commercial Practices policy, which folded Meta's fraud, scams, and deceptive-practices rules into one policy covering ads and organic content alike: misleading financial claims, get-rich-quick offers, products that materially differ from what was advertised, subscriptions without clear disclosure, and celebrity or brand bait. Meta reported removing over 159 million scam ads in 2025, ninety-two percent taken down before anyone reported them, their figures. Circumventing review, cloaking landing pages, redirect chains that hide destinations, multiple accounts to evade restrictions, remains prohibited on its own. And Meta has said it wants verified advertisers driving ninety percent of ad revenue by the end of 2026, so expect identity verification prompts, especially in finance, crypto, investment education, and work-from-home adjacent categories, even for legitimate advertisers.

Restricted categories are allowed with conditions, and this is where most unexpected rejections happen. Online gambling, dating, cryptocurrency, addiction treatment, and CBD all require written permission, certification such as LegitScript, or both, with age gating and local-law compliance. Alcohol must comply with local age minimums that vary by country, so cross-border campaigns risk rejection when delivery leaks into stricter markets. Financial and insurance products must target eighteen-plus and must not directly request personal identifying information in ads. Political and social issue ads require authorization and a paid-for-by disclaimer outside the European Union, are stored in the Ad Library for seven years, and since October 6, 2025 are not deliverable in the EU at all under the bloc's political advertising transparency regulation. Special ad categories, housing, employment, and financial products, require self-identification for advertisers targeting the US, Canada, and parts of Europe, and carry restricted targeting: no exclusion by age, gender, or zip code, and limited detailed targeting, rules that exist to prevent discriminatory delivery.

One policy rewrite deserves its own note because it reversed a long-standing rejection trigger. In July 2026 Meta rewrote its Health and Wellness policy: before-and-after transformations for cosmetic products and procedures are now explicitly allowed for eighteen-plus audiences, and the old blanket ban on weight-loss before-and-after images is gone. The image is no longer the trigger; the claims around it are. Still rejected: statements of inferiority about physical appearance, pinched-fat close-ups, clickbait promising a specific outcome in a set timeframe without qualifiers, wearable-only results, and transformations presented as typical. Adsuploader's one-line summary: before-and-after is allowed again for adult audiences, but inferiority framing, pinched-fat close-ups, and timed-outcome promises still fail review.

Misleading claims and income promises

The misleading-outcomes rule catches exaggerated results, fake urgency, phantom functionality, and bait pricing, and Adsuploader's fixes share one shape: describe the product and the realistic mechanism, not a guaranteed number; use only urgency that is real; show only what the product actually does; state the real cost or condition in the ad itself. Their reviewer test: would a reasonable person feel misled after clicking; if the answer is maybe, soften the claim and move the proof to a landing page you control, remembering that Meta reviews the landing page too. Income claims get their own line because money-making offers overlap with scams: specific guaranteed earnings, passive income on autopilot, and recruitment angles that lead with earnings are banned; realistic descriptions of a business model or skill, where the outcome depends on the user's effort and that is stated honestly, are allowed. Their practical test: remove the dollar figure and the guarantee; if the ad collapses without the income promise, it will not pass review.

When the account, not the ad, is restricted

AdNestly (November 3, 2026) and Maxme (September 25, 2026) converge on the recovery discipline. First, identify what was actually restricted, because a notice about one asset does not mean everything is disabled: a rejected ad is an edit; a disabled ad account stops some or all advertising; a restricted business portfolio blocks everything it owns; an unpublished Page cannot run ads; a restricted personal profile blocks you, and any ad account where you are the sole user may go down with it. Meta may also apply partial restrictions, lowering daily spending limits or removing payment features, and a daily spending limit reached message is not a ban at all, just a cap that resets and rises with history. Maxme's causes list from auditing real accounts is reassuringly ordinary: a card that fails at the payment threshold, a new account nobody secured, and products local law or Meta treat as sensitive. Most are fixable in an afternoon; only a policy restriction needs a review and patience.

The recovery protocol, combining both sources. Fix the cause before asking Meta to look again, whether it is a failed card, a missing two-factor setting, or a claim in an ad, and never claim you fixed something you did not. If security is in question, change passwords, remove unknown users and apps, review active sessions, and enable two-factor authentication before submitting anything. Preserve evidence: dated screenshots of rejection notices, ad copy and creative, landing page URLs, invoices and receipts, and support case numbers, organized in one folder. Then submit one clear, concise review request through Account Quality or Business Support Home from an authorized administrator, containing the account ID, business details, what changed, and a request for manual review; Meta says reviews typically take about forty-eight hours, per Maxme's citation of its help center. Avoid repeated form submissions, aggressive language, and anyone promising guaranteed reinstatement, because Meta, not a third-party recovery service, makes the final decision. And the warning both sources stress: do not create a replacement ad account to get around a restriction, because a new account connected to the same portfolio, card, domain, Page, or profile looks like an attempt to evade enforcement, which is itself prohibited.

What this means for your operation

A working account-health practice emerges from these sources. Build a pre-launch compliance pass into production: check copy for second-person attribute framing, claims for guarantees and timed outcomes, the category for permission or age-gating requirements, and the landing page for the same standard the ad is held to. Secure the account like infrastructure, with two-factor authentication on every administrator and a review of connected apps, because an unsecured account is a restriction waiting to happen. Keep the evidence folder current so a restriction is a process, not a panic. And treat policy as a moving surface: the July 2026 health rewrite and the November window changes this year alone show that what was banned last year may be allowed now and vice versa, so verify the current Standards before relying on any remembered rule, ours included. In our work managing paid campaigns, we run this compliance pass on every campaign we ship and handle the appeal process when restrictions land, because an account that stays healthy is worth more than any single campaign. That is our operating interpretation of the sources above, not a claim from them, and it is work we can do with you.
