How Facebook Recommends Posts, Videos and Pages

A separate AI system shows people content from accounts they never followed. Here is how recommendation ranking works, what is excluded, and how a Page earns its place.

Most of what you see on Facebook comes from accounts you chose to follow. But a growing share comes from accounts you never followed at all: a reel from a creator across the world, a post from a Page you have never heard of, a group suggestion that happens to fit your interests. That content is selected by a separate AI system with its own rules, and for a business Page it is the only organic route to people who do not know you yet. This article explains how Facebook's recommendation system works, what it predicts, what it excludes, and how a Page earns a place inside it.

The mechanics below come from Meta's Facebook Feed Recommendations AI system card, the Facebook Feed system card, and Meta's enforcement documentation. The practical sections are our interpretation and are labeled as such.

Two systems behind one Feed

Meta documents two distinct AI systems that fill your Feed. The first, described in the Facebook Feed system card, ranks connected content: posts from friends, Pages you follow and groups you joined. It gathers everything those sources posted, filters out content flagged for violating the Community Standards, then runs a lightweight model to select roughly 500 candidate posts, scores each one, and orders your Feed by that relevance score while keeping a balanced mix of content types.

The second system, described in the Facebook Feed Recommendations system card, handles unconnected content: public photos, videos, links, reels, events and live video from accounts you have no relationship with. It predicts what you are most likely to be interested in or engage with, based on what and whom you have followed, liked or engaged with recently. Ads sit outside both systems. Understanding which system carries your post matters because they obey different eligibility rules, and the recommendations system is the stricter one.

The recommendation pipeline, step by step

Step 1: Gathering an eligible inventory

The system starts by gathering a portion of the public content available on Facebook. Two filters apply before any ranking happens. Content that goes against the Community Standards is excluded, exactly as in the connected Feed. But recommended content must also clear Meta's Recommendation Guidelines, a second, stricter bar that exists only for unconnected distribution. A post can be perfectly allowed to exist on Facebook and still be ineligible to be recommended to new audiences.

Step 2: Reading signals

For each candidate, the AI considers input signals about the content and about the specific viewer: how that person engaged with similar content, their interests on Facebook, and their recent behavior. Signals are dynamic; Meta says both the models and their inputs change frequently as the system learns. There is no fixed checklist to optimize against, only families of behavior the system keeps measuring.

Step 3: Making predictions

Dedicated models then predict which public content each viewer will find most relevant and valuable. Meta publishes the significant predictions and their input signals openly, and the next section walks through the ones that matter most for a publisher. Every post is scored per person, not once globally: the same reel can be a strong recommendation for one viewer and invisible to another.

Step 4: Ranking and delivery

Finally the system ranks the scored candidates and inserts the winners into the Feed, between connected posts and ads. Content predicted to provide more value for that specific person appears higher. This is the step where your Page's post either enters a stranger's Feed or stays unseen by them, and it happens viewer by viewer, millions of times a day.

The predictions that decide recommendation ranking

Attention predictions

Several models estimate how long a viewer will spend on a recommended post. Signals include the content type, the length of a video, how long the person has already been viewing the triggered post, and the timestamp of the session. A deeper version looks at how many seconds the viewer spent on each topic, how many video posts they watched by topic, and their mobile viewing duration patterns. Attention is measured per topic and per format, which is why a viewer who skips your talking-head videos may still watch your screen recordings to the end.

Sharing predictions, public and private

One model predicts whether the viewer will share the post, using signals like how frequently they share, how often they shared posts from that specific creator, and their topic-level sharing over the last 30 days and 180 days. A separate model predicts private sharing off Facebook: how often the person shared posts on Messenger in the past month, their sharing frequency over one week, one month and three months, and whose posts they shared on WhatsApp in the last 180 days. Content that travels through private messages is a documented positive prediction, which is the mechanical basis for the advice that sendable content outperforms merely likeable content.

The screenshot prediction

The most telling model in the card predicts how likely the viewer is to take a screenshot of the post. Its signals include whose posts the person screenshot over the past 180 days, which specific posts they captured, how many times the post was saved, how many times it was viewed, and how the viewer engaged with different ad formats. Meta treats the screenshot as a marker of value: people capture what they intend to keep, reference or resend. Saves sit in the same family. Content built to be kept, such as checklists, prices, dates and reference steps, feeds exactly this prediction.

Negative-feedback predictions

The system also predicts rejection. One model estimates how likely the viewer is to click the X on a recommended post, using signals such as how many recommended posts they viewed in the last day, 7 days and 14 days, and their total recent view counts. A dismissal is not a neutral event; it is a scored input that pushes similar content down for that viewer. Recommendations live under a constant test: every impression is a chance to be explicitly dismissed.

Community and event predictions

Recommendations extend beyond posts. One model predicts how likely the viewer is to join a suggested group, from signals like their open and closed group memberships, the Pages they liked overall and in the last week, and topics inferred from their group engagement. Another predicts whether they will click RSVP on an event, using their interactions with event posts over 28 days and their approximate distance from the venue. Local proximity is a documented input, which matters for any business whose events, offers or services are geographic.

What keeps content out of recommendations

Meta's problematic-content documentation lists the categories whose distribution it reduces, and they map directly onto recommendation eligibility: clickbait and engagement bait, links to websites covered with ads, slow to load or broken, low-quality comments that are repeatedly copied and pasted, content that is principally repurposed from other sources, videos that abuse the video or live formats, and misinformation. The stated goal is to set incentives toward original, high-quality material, and the disincentives apply before any ranking score is calculated.

The account behind the content matters too. Meta's strike system escalates from a warning through feature restrictions to permanent disablement, and it documents a specific consequence for repeat violators: Pages and groups that repeatedly violate policies are removed from recommendations and have their distribution reduced. A Page with a violations history is not just risking removals; it is silently exiting the discovery system that brings new audiences. Page Quality shows where your Page stands.

Viewer controls shape recommendations too

Viewers hold documented controls over recommended content, and each one is a ranking input. Show more and Show less temporarily increase or decrease the ranking score for that post and posts like it, giving the viewer a direct dial on their own recommendations. Viewers can also report content, and they can bypass personalization entirely: the search feature in the Feeds tab returns non-personalized results based only on the search term. A Page that earns Show more taps is training the system in its favor, one viewer at a time.

What this means for your Page

This section is our interpretation of the documented mechanics, not Meta's advice:

  • Publish for strangers, not only followers. Recommendation eligibility is the gate to non-follower reach, so every post should be one a person with no context can value on its own.
  • Build keepable content. Screenshots and saves are documented value predictions, so reference material such as steps, prices, dates and templates earns distribution that opinions do not.
  • Design for the private share. The system predicts Messenger and WhatsApp sharing separately, so ask whether a friend would forward this specific post to one specific person.
  • Protect your eligibility. Engagement bait, clickbait and repurposed content are documented demotion categories, and repeated violations remove Pages from recommendations entirely.
  • Respect the dismissal dial. Every impression carries a predicted X-click risk, so irrelevant frequency teaches viewers to dismiss you and trains the system to stop offering your content.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming recommendations are free reach. The inventory filter is stricter than the Community Standards, and borderline content that survives on your Page never enters the recommendation pool.
  • Chasing follower-only engagement. Likes from existing followers feed the connected Feed system; recommendations respond to per-viewer predictions from people who have never met your Page.
  • Ignoring originality. Content principally repurposed from other sources is a named demotion category, so reposting trends without added value caps your discovery.
  • Letting violations accumulate. Strikes escalate quietly, and the recommendation removal penalty arrives before most Pages notice the pattern in their reach.
  • Optimizing for a fixed signal list. Meta states the models and signals change frequently, so durable quality beats any frozen checklist.

A worked example

A Cairo furniture workshop posts two kinds of reels. The first kind is a polished showroom pan with a trending audio track and a caption asking viewers to tag someone. It collects quick views from existing followers and dies there. The second kind is a 40-second repair tutorial: how to fix a wobbly chair leg with two tools, priced in Egyptian pounds, with the steps written in the caption. Viewers save it, screenshot the materials list, and forward it to family group chats on WhatsApp.

Under the documented predictions, the second reel feeds the screenshot and save models, the private-sharing model and the attention models with full watch-throughs from strangers. It also carries no engagement bait, so nothing blocks its eligibility. Weeks later it still appears in the Feeds of people who never heard of the workshop, because recommendation ranking re-evaluates strong content for each new viewer rather than treating a post as a one-day event. The first reel asked for a signal; the second earned several at once, and the system can tell the difference.

Where to start this week

Take your best-performing post of the last month and ask one question about it: would a stranger save this, screenshot it, or send it to one specific person? If the honest answer is no, rebuild the idea into something keepable: a checklist, a price list, a three-step fix, a local guide. Publish it as a reel or a photo post with the value fully visible in the content itself rather than behind a link. Then check Page Quality to confirm nothing is limiting your eligibility, and repeat weekly. Recommendations compound slowly; the Pages that win them are the ones that treat every post as an audition in front of someone who owes them nothing.

Frequently asked questions

Are recommendations the same as the Facebook algorithm?

They are one of two documented systems. The Feed ranking system orders content from accounts you follow; the recommendations system selects public content from accounts you do not. Both use predictions and signals, but eligibility rules are stricter for recommendations.

Can I pay to enter recommendations?

No. Ads are a separate system that Meta explicitly excludes from both Feed system cards. Recommendation placement is earned through eligibility and predicted value, and no budget buys it.

Why did my reach from recommendations suddenly drop?

The documented causes to check first are Page Quality violations, content in a demoted category such as engagement bait or unoriginal repurposing, and viewer dismissal signals. Meta also states the models change frequently, so gradual drift is normal while sudden drops usually have a specific cause.

Do hashtags or keywords control recommendations?

The published signal lists are dominated by behavioral records: what each viewer watched, saved, shared and dismissed. Text signals exist, but the documented system is built on measured behavior, not on the words you stuff into a caption.
