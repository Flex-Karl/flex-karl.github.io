Instagram Originality and Recommendation Eligibility: The Documented Rules

Instagram's documented eligibility gate: original content, no watermarks, recommendation guidelines, and the ten-repost aggregator line. How the rules work and how to stay recommendable.

Two questions decide whether Instagram shows your content to people who do not follow you: is it eligible to be recommended, and is it original enough to keep that eligibility. Instagram has answered both publicly. In April 2024 the company announced four changes to how recommendations treat original content, copies and aggregators, and its creator documentation states the eligibility conditions in plain language. A separate AI system card, updated in June 2026, describes how recommended content is ranked after it passes the gate. This article assembles that documented picture: the eligibility conditions, the originality rules with their thresholds and exceptions, the recommendation guidelines categories, and the account status tool that tells you where you stand. The sources are Instagram's own pages, linked below with their dates; where we extend the documentation into practice, we say so.

The documented eligibility gate

Instagram's creator documentation defines eligible recommended content with four conditions: it is original, it does not violate the Community Guidelines, it has no visible watermarks, and it satisfies the recommendation guidelines. Read it as a gate, not a score. Every piece of eligible content, the documentation says, is shown to a small audience Instagram thinks will enjoy it, regardless of whether they follow the account. The top performing set is then shown to a slightly wider audience, the best of those to a wider group still, and so on. Two consequences follow. The staged ladder is documented fact: distribution in recommendations is earned audience by audience, so the response of each small test audience determines whether the next stage opens. And failing the gate is absolute, our reading of the structure: no ranking craft compensates for ineligibility, because ineligible content never enters the ladder. A watermark from another app, a category the recommendation guidelines exclude, or an originality problem each close the gate before ranking begins.

The four changes of April 2024

The April 30, 2024 announcement, published on Instagram's creator site, names four changes and the reason for them: historically, creators with large followings and aggregators that share copies of original content got more reach in recommendations than smaller original creators, and Instagram wanted to correct that. First, a new input to ranking gives smaller creators more distribution. Second, when Instagram finds two or more identical pieces of content, it recommends only the original, which directly replaces the copy in recommendation surfaces. Third, copied versions receive a label linking to the original creator. Fourth, content aggregators become ineligible for recommendations once they cross a defined threshold of reposts. The four changes work as one system: originals are identified, promoted, credited, and protected from accounts built on republishing them.

How Instagram decides which copy is the original

The replacement rule comes with documented boundaries. Instagram applies it when the original content is relatively new and the match is confident, based on audio and visual signals. It applies only in places where content is recommended: Explore, Reels surfaces and in-feed recommendations, not in what followers see from accounts they chose to follow. When a replacement happens, the original creator is notified. The label mechanism runs alongside it: a copied version carries a link to the original creator, visible to the followers of the account that copied it, and for now either the original creator or the copying account can remove the label from the three-dots menu on the content. The documentation does not publish the matching threshold beyond audio and visual signals, so claims about fooling the matcher with crops or filters have no documented basis, in either direction.

The material enhancement exception

Replacement does not apply to content changed in a significant way. The documentation names four examples: content materially edited to become a meme, a parody compilation, narrated with a new voiceover, or remixed to express a reaction. Our reading: the documented test is transformation, not effort, and the four examples share a pattern, the copy now says something the original did not. The documentation does not enumerate what fails the test, so we would not treat light edits, reframes or watermark removal as safe; nothing official supports that. When in doubt, the safe structure is the one the examples share: add a layer of expression that is yours.

The aggregator threshold: ten reposts in thirty days

The aggregator rule is the most specific line in the documentation. Accounts that repeatedly, ten or more times in the last thirty days, post content from other Instagram users that they did not create or enhance in a material way will not be shown in surfaces where Instagram recommends content. Eligibility returns when the account drops below the threshold, calculated on a rolling basis. Two documented carve-outs matter. Publishers Instagram has identified as holding licensing agreements or explicit permissions from content creators are not affected. And the rule touches recommendation surfaces only: it does not change how content from these accounts appears to people who follow them. The documentation also separates this rule from intellectual property policy, which independently forbids posting content that violates another user's rights; a repost can clear the aggregator count and still infringe copyright.

The second gate: recommendation guidelines

Eligibility has a second layer beyond originality. Instagram's recommendation guidelines hold recommended content to a higher standard than the Community Guidelines, because recommendations reach people who never chose to see the account, including people as young as thirteen. The documentation lists categories allowed on the platform but potentially ineligible for recommendation: content that impedes a safe community, such as discussion of self-harm, suicide or eating disorders, depictions of violence like fighting, sexually suggestive material, and promotion of regulated products such as tobacco, vaping, adult services or pharmaceuticals; sensitive or low-quality health and finance content, such as cosmetic procedure promotion, exaggerated health claims or miracle cures, and misleading business models such as payday loans; political content related to laws, elections or social topics; clickbait and engagement bait; unoriginal content largely repurposed from another source without material value; and false or misleading content, including claims found false by independent fact-checkers and promotion of fraudulent documents. A post that violates these guidelines is avoided where content is recommended, and an account that repeatedly posts such content can become ineligible for recommendation for a period of time. Users also hold documented controls, the sensitive content control and the political content control, that tune how much of some categories they see.

Account status: the documented control panel

Instagram documents account status as the place to understand what content may be ineligible for recommendations, remove the content affecting the account, and appeal a decision you believe is wrong. The aggregator threshold is checked there, and the appeal route exists for it as well. Our practical reading: account status is the only documented channel through which Instagram tells you about recommendation eligibility before or after the fact, and the appeal option makes it the first stop when recommended reach collapses without an obvious cause. The diagnostics in our reach article pair with this: rule the eligibility gate in or out first, then investigate ranking.

How eligible content earns distribution

For content that passes the gate, the Instagram Feed Recommendations system card, updated June 2026, describes the ranking stage. The system gathers a portion of public content that follows quality and integrity rules, considers input signals, and ranks by predicted value for each viewer. Among its significant predictions is one worth sitting with: how informative a post is, a signal the card describes as purely generated by an LLM to assess the post's content quality. Other documented predictions measure the likelihood a viewer taps Interested, clicks the author's profile, comments, or keeps scrolling, with signals such as how many impressions you have made on recommended content and how often other viewers visited the author's profile after seeing the post. Our reading of the set: recommendation ranking measures satisfied curiosity, a viewer who taps through to the profile or comments gave the system evidence the recommendation worked, and a documented model now grades content quality directly. Users shape it from their side too: Not interested, hide, snooze for recommendations, word filters and a Following feed with no recommendations are all documented controls.

What this means for a content strategy

Our synthesis of the documented rules, and it is ours. Originality is no longer a values statement; it is infrastructure. An account built on reposting now carries a measurable ceiling, ten copies in thirty days, after which its recommendation reach switches off until the count rolls down. An account built on original work gets the reverse: protection of its reach through replacement and labels, plus the staged ladder that gives small accounts a documented entry point. The planning consequence is a budget question. Every repost spends against the threshold; every original reel replenishes nothing and risks nothing. Accounts that curate for a living can stay under the line deliberately, but they should count, because the system is counting.

Common mistakes

  • Reposting with credit and assuming credit equals originality. The documented aggregator threshold counts reposts of others' content, and attribution is not among the documented exemptions.
  • Assuming any edit makes a copy safe. The documentation protects content changed in a significant way and names examples like memes, parody compilations, new voiceovers and reaction remixes; it documents no safe minimum of editing.
  • Treating allowed as recommendable. Entire categories, from regulated products to engagement bait to political content, are permitted on the platform yet potentially excluded from recommendations.
  • Leaving visible watermarks from other apps on reels. No visible watermarks is a documented eligibility condition, so the clean master file is the one to upload.
  • Ignoring account status until reach collapses. It is the documented surface for eligibility problems, removal and appeal, and it catches issues while they are still cheap to fix.
  • Confusing follower reach with recommendation reach. The replacement, label and aggregator rules apply where content is recommended; what followers see is unchanged, so a healthy home feed can hide a dead discovery pipeline.

A worked example

A kitchenware retailer in Alexandria ran its Instagram presence on compilations: trending recipe reels from other accounts, reposted with short captions, fifteen to twenty times a month. Follower engagement looked fine. Then recommended reach faded to nearly nothing, and account status showed the account ineligible for recommendations: the rolling count of unenhanced reposts had crossed ten in thirty days, exactly the documented threshold. The recovery followed the documentation. The reposts stopped, original reels replaced them, simple forty-second clips of the products in use in the shop's own kitchen, and the few compilations that remained were rebuilt as reaction pieces with the owner's own voiceover, the documented pattern of material enhancement.

Within weeks the rolling count dropped below ten, account status cleared, and the original reels began entering the staged ladder the documentation describes: small test audiences first, then wider ones as completion and profile taps accumulated. One reel, a two-ingredient dough demo, reached an audience several times the account's follower count through recommendations alone, the pattern the small-creator ranking input was built to allow. The example illustrates the documented mechanics; it is not a controlled study.

Where to start this week

Open account status and read it before anything else; it is the documented source of truth for your eligibility, and the appeal route lives there. Then audit your last thirty days of posts against the aggregator line: count the pieces you did not create or materially enhance, because the system counts them on the same rolling window. Rebuild your upload habit around clean masters, no watermarks from other apps, and a calendar where originals outnumber reposts by a wide margin. If curation is part of your model, keep it under the threshold and add a transformation layer, your voiceover, your reaction, your compilation thesis, that the documentation recognizes as material. Eligibility is the gate; everything you know about retention, saves and sends only works after you pass through it.

Frequently asked questions

Does reposting with credit protect my account?

The documentation lists no attribution exemption. The aggregator threshold counts content from other Instagram users that you did not create or enhance in a material way, whether or not you credit them. Credit is good practice and the label mechanism may add it automatically, but it does not change the count.

What counts as enhancing content in a material way?

The documentation names examples rather than a test: material edits that turn content into a meme, a parody compilation, a piece narrated with a new voiceover, or a remix expressing a reaction. The shared pattern is transformation that adds your own expression. No exhaustive definition is documented, so borderline edits are a risk you carry, not a rule you can cite.

My account became ineligible for recommendations. Can it recover?

The documentation says aggregator accounts become eligible again when they drop below ten unenhanced reposts in the rolling thirty-day window, and account status shows the status, lets you remove affecting content, and offers an appeal. For recommendation guidelines violations, repeated posting can make an account ineligible for a period of time, with the same account status route to diagnose, remove and appeal.

Do these rules change what my followers see?

No. The documentation scopes replacement, labels and aggregator ineligibility to surfaces where content is recommended, such as Explore, Reels and in-feed recommendations. Content from an account people follow still reaches them as before.

Is political or sensitive content banned from Instagram?

The documented line is narrower. Specific categories, political content among them, are allowed on the platform but may be ineligible for recommendation, and users hold controls that tune how much of some categories they see in recommendations. Banned and not recommended are different outcomes with different remedies.
