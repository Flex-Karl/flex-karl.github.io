Facebook Content Distribution: Original Content vs Reposted Content

Meta now strips monetization and distribution from repeated reposts while duplicate detection protects originals. Here is what counts as original and how to build on it.

Two Pages can publish the same video on Facebook and get opposite outcomes: one earns distribution and monetization, the other quietly loses both. The difference is rarely production quality. It is whether the system classifies the post as original content or as a repost, and in 2025 Meta turned that classification from a background ranking signal into an enforcement program with published consequences. This article explains what Meta counts as original, what the July 2025 crackdown actually changed, and how a business Page builds distribution on material it truly owns.

The mechanics below come from Meta's Transparency Center documentation and Meta's July 2025 announcement as reported by The Verge and CNBC. We label what is official documentation, what is reported announcement, and what is our interpretation.

What Meta means by original and unoriginal content

Meta's definition, quoted in its July 2025 announcement, is specific: unoriginal content is images or videos reused without crediting the original creator. Two edges of that definition matter. First, the problem is the missing credit and the missing contribution, not the reuse itself. Meta stated explicitly that the changes should not affect creators who add their unique take when they reshare content, add commentary in a reaction video, or join a viral trend. Second, the target is repeated behavior: the penalties land on accounts that repeatedly reuse someone else's videos, photos or text posts.

Original content, by contrast, is not defined as a format but as a relationship between creator and material: something you filmed, wrote, recorded or substantially made yours. Meta's own best-practice guidance for creators who build on existing material is to add meaningful edits, voiceovers or commentary, and to avoid visible third-party watermarks and content visibly recycled from other apps. A watermark from another platform is treated as a fingerprint of reposting, and it is one of the easiest signals for any detection system to read.

The July 2025 crackdown, in numbers

In July 2025 Meta announced a coordinated push against unoriginal and spammy content on Facebook. As reported by CNBC, the company said it had removed about 10 million profiles in the first half of 2025 for impersonating large content producers, and had taken action against roughly 500,000 accounts engaged in spammy behavior or fake engagement, demoting their comments and reducing the distribution of their content. These are Meta's own figures from its announcement, not independent measurements.

For ordinary reposting rather than impersonation, the announced consequences are different but still material. As reported by The Verge, creators who repeatedly reuse someone else's videos, photos or text posts lose access to Facebook monetization programs for a period of time and see reduced distribution of their posts. Meta also said its systems now detect duplicate videos and reduce the distribution of copies so they do not take views from the original creator, and that it is testing features that add links directing viewers to the original content. The direction is unambiguous: distribution and money follow the source, not the copy.

The rules were there before the crackdown

The July announcement did not invent the originality standard; it gave it teeth. Meta's problematic-content documentation has long named limited originality content, meaning material principally repurposed from other sources, as a category whose distribution it reduces, alongside low-quality videos that abuse the video or live formats. The same documentation frames the goal as setting incentives toward high-quality material. And as covered in our article on recommendations, any content entering the recommendation system must clear the Recommendation Guidelines, a stricter bar than the Community Standards. Reposted content could already survive on a Page yet remain invisible to non-followers; the crackdown extended the consequences to monetization and account-level distribution.

How distribution actually differs between original and reposted

Inside the ranking systems, originality operates at three layers. At the eligibility layer, duplicate or clearly recycled material can be excluded from recommendations before any scoring happens. At the scoring layer, the Feed and recommendation systems measure per-viewer behavior, and copies compete against the original's accumulated history: the first upload of a popular video holds the watch time, shares and comment threads that copies lack. At the enforcement layer, duplicate-video detection suppresses copies directly, and repeated reposting removes monetization and reduces distribution account-wide. A repost is therefore not just a weaker post; it is a post fighting with one hand tied, inside an account that is quietly losing standing.

The gray zone: curation, commentary and trends

Most business Pages are not pirates or studios; they live in the gray zone of curated content. Meta's stated position gives the zone a workable map. Resharing with your unique take is acceptable. Reaction formats with real commentary are acceptable. Joining a trend is acceptable. What fails is the copy with nothing added: the viral clip reuploaded as-is, the meme posted for the tenth time, the competitor's photo with the logo cropped out. The documented test to apply before publishing is not did we change the file but did we change the value: does a viewer get something from our version that the original does not give them?

How to check whether your Page is affected

You can inspect your standing directly, and the check takes minutes:

  • Open Page Quality from your Page's professional dashboard. It lists content violations, restrictions and anything currently limiting your Page, including removal from recommendations.
  • Review your monetization status in the professional dashboard if you use any monetization program; the announced penalties suspend access for a period of time, and the status page shows where you stand.
  • Compare reach sources in your insights. A healthy original catalog earns a share of reach from non-followers through recommendations; if non-follower reach has collapsed to near zero while nothing else changed, eligibility is the first thing to suspect.
  • Scan your recent posts for the recycling signals Meta names: third-party watermarks, other apps' aspect ratios, burned-in captions, and reuploads of clips you did not film. Each one is a fixable liability.

If you find restrictions, the documented path is to correct the underlying behavior and, where Meta offers it, request a review through the same Page Quality surface. There is no separate originality appeal; standing recovers as the pattern of posting changes.

What this means for your Page

This section is our interpretation of the documented mechanics and reported announcement, not Meta's advice:

  • Anchor your calendar in material you own. Even simple original footage, shot on a phone in your shop, enters the system without any of the eligibility and enforcement handicaps a repost carries.
  • If you curate, transform. Voiceover, commentary, editing and local context are the documented markers of an acceptable unique take; a straight reupload has none of them.
  • Strip nothing, credit everything. Reused material without credit fits the announced definition of unoriginal content, while attribution moves you toward the accepted curation pattern.
  • Never post watermarked clips. Visible third-party watermarks are named in Meta's own guidance as a repost fingerprint.
  • Think account-level, not post-level. The announced penalties for repeated reuse are monetization loss and account-wide distribution reduction, so a pattern of reposts taxes even your original work.

Common mistakes

  • Assuming everyone does it is a defense. The July announcement targets repeated reuse precisely because it is common, and enforcement is account-level.
  • Editing cosmetically. Cropping, filters and speed changes do not add the meaningful edits or commentary the guidance names; they decorate a copy instead of transforming it.
  • Confusing legal with distributable. Content you have every right to post can still be demoted as limited-originality material, because distribution rules are stricter than ownership rules.
  • Building reach on trends you do not feed. A Page whose reach comes from reposted viral clips loses that reach the moment duplicate detection catches up, and it has no original catalog to fall back on.
  • Ignoring watermarks and recycling tells. Watermarks, aspect ratios from other apps and burned-in captions are all visible recycling signals that Meta's guidance tells creators to avoid.

A worked example

A kitchenware shop in Alexandria runs two content strategies in sequence. For three months it reposts satisfying cooking clips found online, cropped square and captioned in Arabic. Reach looks healthy for a while, then decays: posts stop appearing beyond existing followers, which is consistent with the recommendation eligibility filter and duplicate detection doing their documented work. Then the shop switches: the owner films ninety-second clips of her own products in her own kitchen, a pot that does not stick, a knife through a tomato, prices on screen, one recipe tip per video.

Under the documented system, the second catalog is original by construction: footage she owns, no watermarks, clear value per clip. It is eligible for recommendations, it accumulates its own watch history instead of competing with an original elsewhere, and it carries zero risk of the announced monetization and distribution penalties. The follower count grows more slowly than the viral clips promised, but the reach is real, repeatable, and hers. That is the actual trade the originality rules offer: slower compounding, but you keep what you build.

Where to start this week

Audit your last twenty posts and sort them honestly: owned footage and writing, transformed curation with real commentary, or straight reposts. Delete or rework the third group, starting with anything carrying another platform's watermark. Then commit to a minimum original ratio for the next month, for example four owned posts for every curated one, and film the first owned clip this week with whatever phone you have. Check Page Quality for any existing strikes while you are at it. The Pages that win distribution under these rules are not the ones with the best footage; they are the ones whose catalog is unambiguously theirs.

Frequently asked questions

Is sharing someone else's post with the share button a repost?

No. A native share preserves the original post and its attribution. The announced definition of unoriginal content targets downloading and reuploading material without credit, which strips attribution and duplicates the content.

Can I still use trending audio or formats?

Yes. Meta stated the changes should not affect creators who join trends or add their unique take. A trend is a shared format; what the system measures is whether your material and contribution are yours.

What happens if my original video gets stolen and reposted?

Meta says duplicate-video detection reduces the distribution of copies so they do not take views from the original, and it is testing links that direct viewers to the original content. You can also report the copy through the standard reporting flow.

Does this apply to text and photos or only videos?

The announced penalties cover videos, photos and text posts. Duplicate detection technology is described specifically for videos, but the monetization and distribution consequences are stated for repeated reuse of all three formats.
