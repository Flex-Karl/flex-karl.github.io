Facebook Ranking Signals: What Determines Content Distribution

Meta uses thousands of signals and over 100 prediction models to order Feed. Here are the real signal categories, the demotion signals, and what they mean for your Page.

Every time someone opens Facebook, the ranking system decides which of the posts available to that person appear first. That decision is not driven by one magic factor. It is built from thousands of small pieces of information called signals, which feed more than one hundred prediction models. Understanding what those signals are, and what they are not, is the difference between a content strategy built on evidence and one built on rumors.

This article maps the signal landscape using Meta's own Transparency Center documentation: what signals are, the categories they fall into, how they become predictions, which ones push content down instead of up, and what all of this means for a business Page in practice.

Signals vs predictions: the distinction that matters

A signal is an input fact. It is something the system can observe: who created the post, what type of media it contains, how you interacted with similar posts before, how long you watched comparable videos. Meta says it considers thousands of signals for every post it ranks.

A prediction is what the system infers from those signals. The Feed ranking system holds over 100 prediction models, and they answer questions like: how likely are you to comment on this post, how likely are you to share it, how much time will you spend watching it, and how likely are other people to respond if you do. Signals are the evidence; predictions are the conclusions drawn from that evidence. The final Feed order comes from combining many predictions into one relevance score per post.

This distinction matters because businesses often talk about signals as if the system simply counts them: more likes means more reach. In reality, a raw count is only an input. What moves a post is what the models predict from everything they know, and those predictions are personalized for each individual viewer.

The major signal categories

Meta publishes the categories that, in its words, represent the vast majority of signals currently used in Feed ranking for connected content. These are the groups, with real examples from the documentation.

Signals about you, the viewer

  • Basic account information: how long you have used Facebook, your language, location-related information such as IP address if you allow it, your time of day, and how many days you were active in a period.
  • How you interact with content: the types of content you engage with and how, for example how often you click photo posts or comment on video posts.
  • How you view content: what types you look at and for how long, such as time spent looking at photos, reading comments, or watching video.

Signals about your connections

The system reads the state of your network: how many friends you have and how often you interact with each one, how many Pages you follow and how many you visited recently, how many Groups you joined, and what new posts are available from all of them, including posts with fresh comment activity.

Signals about the post itself

  • Attributes: the privacy setting, and whether the post contains photos, video, Live video or a link.
  • Content: if it contains a URL, the ratio of that domain's popularity on Facebook versus the internet as a whole; the percentage of identical content between this post and other posts; whether it likely contains nudity, graphic violence or other probable Community Standards violations; and whether independent fact-checkers rated it false or partially false.
  • Media: dimensions of shared photos, what visuals they contain, and whether a video loops or is a still image.
  • Topic: what the post is about.

Signals about who created and shared it

The actor behind the post has its own signal set: whether they posted to a profile, Page or Group, how many confirmed Community Standards violations the account carries, how much they have posted recently, and how other users have viewed and interacted with their content before, including total followers, Page views, shares, comments and likes over a period. When the person who shared the post is different from its creator, both actors get evaluated.

Signals about your relationship to the post

Finally, the system looks at the intersection: whether you or a friend administers the Page that shared the post, how you have viewed and interacted with this actor's content in the past, and your total history with similar content types. This is why the same post can rank first for one person and never surface for another.

From signals to predictions

Meta groups its 100-plus prediction models into four families: predictions about actions you will take on the post, predictions about how you will spend time viewing it, predictions about your interest in the post or in the person, Page or Group behind it, and predictions about how others will interact with the post if you take an action such as commenting or sharing.

The models used most frequently in ranking include how likely you are to click on some part of the post, to meaningfully interact with it through some combination of commenting, liking, reacting, sharing to Messenger, resharing or spending time on it, to share it, to spend time viewing it or its comments, to visit the Page afterward, to watch its video and for how long, and whether you will want to see more or less content from whoever shared it.

Meta also runs surveys asking people whether a post was worth their time, and uses the answers to train predictions about what other content you will find worthwhile. No single prediction is treated as a perfect measure of value, which is why the system combines many of them, with a stated goal of making the app valuable in the long term rather than in one scroll.

The practical consequence: because ranking is personalized, the same signal can weigh differently for different people and formats. Predicted watch time may matter more for a video post, while predicted clicks matter more for a link post. For someone who never taps Like, time spent reading becomes the more useful prediction.

Signals that reduce distribution

Not every signal pushes content up. Several prediction models exist specifically to reduce distribution: how likely you are to hide, snooze or unsubscribe from a post, how likely you are to report it, and how likely you are to react with anger, which Meta explicitly says is used to reduce distribution. Posts rated false or partially false by independent fact-checking partners lose reach, as does content the system flags as a likely Community Standards violation.

Beyond Feed itself, Meta uses ranking to reduce what it calls borderline content: material that does not quite break the rules but disappoints. Its own example is a headline that intentionally withholds information to lure people into clicking, the classic clickbait pattern. Meta is deliberately less detailed about this subset of signals, to guard against bad actors gaming them.

What this means for your business Page

The sourced facts above translate into a small set of durable principles. This part is our interpretation of Meta's documentation, not Meta's advice:

  • Publish original content. The system measures the percentage of identical content between your post and other posts, so recycled material starts with a handicap.
  • Aim for conversation, not applause. The most-used predictions include meaningful interaction and the discussion your post sparks, including the additional comments and replies your engagement triggers.
  • Make video worth finishing. Predicted watch time and predicted viewing time are core models, so retention beats raw views.
  • Never use clickbait or engagement bait. Headlines that withhold information to force clicks and posts that beg for reactions feed the demotion models, not the ranking ones.
  • Build a real audience over time. Interest predictions lean on your past engagement with each person, so consistent value for the same followers compounds; rented reach does not.

Common mistakes

  • Hunting for one most important signal. Meta states the combination of all models is what matters, and their relative impact varies per person and per content type.
  • Treating the signal list as fixed. Meta describes signals and models as dynamic and changing frequently as the system learns, so any static cheat sheet ages fast.
  • Reposting the same creative across Pages. Identical-content percentage is a measured signal, and near-duplicate posts compete with themselves.
  • Optimizing for reactions the system discounts. Angry reactions and bait patterns are inputs to demotion predictions, not growth levers.
  • Ignoring what viewers tell the system. Hides, snoozes and reports are strong negative predictions; content that earns them trains the models against you.

A worked example

Imagine two Cairo bakery Pages posting the same morning. Page A uploads an original 45-second video of its baladi bread coming out of the oven, asks viewers which filling they would try, and answers every comment. Page B reposts a trending recipe video that already circulates on dozens of Pages, with a caption urging people to like and share.

For a follower who usually watches food videos to the end, the models will likely predict high watch time and meaningful interaction for Page A's post: original content, an active comment thread, a strong viewing history match. For Page B, the identical-content signal flags the video as a copy, and the engagement-bait caption aligns with patterns the demotion systems exist to catch. Same niche, same hour, opposite trajectories, and nothing about it required luck.

Frequently asked questions

Do likes still matter for reach?

Yes, but as one input among many. Predicted likes appear in Meta's occasionally-used model group, while meaningful interaction, sharing, viewing time and interest predictions sit in the most-used group. A like helps; it does not decide.

Is there a public list of every signal?

No. Meta publishes the categories that represent the vast majority of signals, with examples, and keeps some detail private, especially around signals used to identify problematic content, to prevent abuse of the system.

Do signals work the same for every viewer?

No. Ranking is personalized, and Meta says the relative impact of each prediction model varies depending on the person and the content. Your post competes inside each follower's individual Feed, not in one global ranking.

Can I see the signals Facebook holds about my Page?

Not directly. What you can do is control your side: publish original content, earn genuine interactions, avoid bait patterns, and use the controls Meta gives viewers, such as Favorites, to your advantage by being worth prioritizing.
