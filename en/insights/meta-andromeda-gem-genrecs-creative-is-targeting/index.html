Andromeda, GEM and GenRecs: How Meta Chooses Ads Now, and Why Creative Is the Targeting

Meta's 2026 ad pipeline: Andromeda retrieval, the GEM foundation model and GenRecs. What each stage does, what Meta's published figures actually say, and why creative diversification replaced interest stacking.

Every time someone opens Facebook or Instagram, Meta chooses one ad from tens of millions in a fraction of a second. The machinery behind that choice changed fundamentally over the last two years, and in October 2026 Meta described the newest version of it on its business site: a rebuilt retrieval engine called Andromeda, a foundation model called GEM, and an LLM-based system called GenRecs. None of these has a setting you can switch on, yet together they decide whether your ad is even considered. Understanding the pipeline changes one practical decision above all: how many genuinely different creative concepts you give the system to work with. This article explains the stages in plain language, separates Meta's published figures from verified fact, and ends with what to change in your next campaign.

The pipeline: retrieval, ranking, auction

Meta cannot score tens of millions of ads with its heaviest models in the milliseconds before a feed renders, so the choice happens in stages. The first stage is retrieval, where Andromeda narrows the eligible pool down to a few thousand candidates that could suit this particular person. The second is ranking, where GEM, Meta's Generative Ads Recommendation Model, scores those candidates on how likely the person is to click, buy or sign up. The third is the auction, where your bid, the predicted value and the competition decide which single ad appears. Alongside these, Meta now runs generative retrieval: instead of only matching existing ads to a person, the system describes the kind of ad that person would respond to and then finds real ads that fit the description. The stage that matters most for advertisers is the first one. If retrieval never shortlists your ad for someone, nothing in your bidding or optimization can recover it.

Andromeda: a smarter first cut

Meta introduced Andromeda in late 2024 as an ads retrieval engine co-designed with its own MTIA accelerators and NVIDIA hardware, claiming a 10,000x increase in model complexity at the retrieval stage. The next generation, described on October 28, 2026 and nicknamed Andromeda 2.0 in the industry though Meta does not use that label, routes each ad to specialized expert models rather than running one general model over everything. Meta says the system now scores every eligible ad at over one billion ads per second, bringing what it calls near ranking-level judgment into the retrieval stage. Its published results: a 2.4% increase in Facebook conversions since 2025, and a further 10x complexity increase in Q3 2026 that Meta credits with 1.37% more conversions on Facebook and 0.24% on Instagram Reels. From the Q3 earnings call on October 21, 2026, industry coverage adds figures for the wider family: generative retrieval lifting conversions nearly 2% in the segments where it runs, and GEM adding 1.7% on off-site conversion campaigns and 9.6% on app ads. All of these are Meta's own numbers without published methods or baselines, so read them as direction, not proof.

GEM: one brain teaching many models

GEM, introduced in March 2025, began as a large generative model for ad recommendations on Facebook Feed and Instagram. Meta now describes it as the foundation model behind ads recommendations across Facebook, Instagram, Reels and off-Meta conversions, trained at LLM scale for every major advertiser objective. Its interesting property is not that it serves your ads directly but that it teaches: through knowledge transfer, what GEM learns centrally is passed down to the real-time ranking models on every surface. Meta says the current generation reads twice as much engagement history through a model 2.5 times more complex, and that campaigns with few conversions benefit from what GEM learns elsewhere, which matters most for deep-funnel objectives like purchases. The published cumulative results, again the vendor's own account: up to 18.9% conversion improvement and 12% click-through improvement across surfaces, with 5.8%, 4.1% and 1.6% conversion uplift on Facebook, Instagram and offsite conversions respectively for targeted users.

GenRecs: teaching the system what your ad is about

The third piece, GenRecs, is Meta's name for enriching its recommendation systems with large language models. The mechanism worth understanding is tokenization: the LLM reads each ad's text and images and describes the ad in semantic tokens that capture attributes like a product's brand, color, fit and style. Those tokens form a shared vocabulary, so a brand-new ad with no engagement history can be related to ads the system already knows well, which is precisely the cold-start problem that used to bury new creative. GenRecs now sits underneath Andromeda: the system generates a semantic ID for the ideal ad for a person, then matches it against the ads available. For advertisers, the implication is blunt. The system increasingly understands your ad as content, so what your ad says and shows, not which interest box you ticked, is what the matching runs on.

Why creative is the targeting now

Meta said the strategic conclusion out loud in its creative diversification guidance: the focus has moved from niche targeting to creative diversification, defined as creating a wide range of ad creatives with different themes, messages and visuals, and it called the constraints you place on your creative process the biggest constraint on your Meta strategy. A smarter retrieval system raises the stakes on that advice. Retrieval matches people to ads, and the ad is what it matches on, so each genuinely different concept is a new way for the system to find a different pocket of people. Five versions of one idea, the same video in five colors, give retrieval one idea to work with. Five different ideas give it five. Some guides claim Meta groups near-identical ads together with precise similarity thresholds; no Meta statement confirms those thresholds, so do not plan around invented numbers, but the practical point stands: near-copies do not reach new people.

What did not change

The auction still exists, and with it everything you already knew about bids, budgets and the learning phase. A stronger shortlist does not rescue an underfunded ad set, and ranking still leans on the quality of your conversion signals: the Pixel and Conversions API setup, event deduplication and catalog hygiene for commerce all feed the very data these models learn from. Automation features like Advantage+ audience and Advantage+ campaign budget sit on top of this pipeline rather than replacing it. And the measurement discipline from the rest of this series still applies: the models optimize toward the events you give them, so a misconfigured event is now a mistake the foundation model learns at scale. The new machinery raises the value of clean inputs; it does not forgive dirty ones.

How to read Meta's performance claims

Every percentage in this article comes from Meta describing its own systems, and Meta publishes no methods, baselines or segment definitions for them. Some figures are cumulative since 2025, some are quarter-specific, some cover only targeted users or the segments where a feature runs. None of that makes the numbers false; it makes them unauditable from outside. The honest way to use them is as a map of where Meta is investing, which tells you where the product will keep moving, not as a promise of what your account will see. Your own before-and-after, read on site-side conversions and cost per result with creative concepts as the variable, remains the only lift figure you should ever budget against in your planning.

What to actually change on Monday

The operating guidance that follows from the mechanics, and from what working agencies report, is consistent. Count concepts, not ads: a concept is a different angle, person, problem, format or offer, while a color swap or a new headline on the same video is the same concept. A useful test is whether you can describe every live ad in one sentence; if you can, you have one concept, not twenty. Make enough of them: practitioners writing about these systems aim for twenty to twenty-five genuinely different concepts per campaign where budget allows, fewer on small budgets because each concept needs spend to be read. Consolidate structure: fewer ad sets with many distinct creatives give retrieval a well-funded pool, which also fits what the learning phase demands. And let delivery tell you which concept to grow: use the creative breakdowns in Ads Manager to see which concept the system backs, brief more versions of that idea, and keep adding new concepts so retrieval always has fresh material.

A fairer start for new ads

One consequence of GenRecs deserves its own note, because it changes launch behavior. Historically, a new ad suffered a cold start: with no engagement history, the system had little to match on, so new creative often died before it gathered the signals that would have saved it. Semantic tokens attack exactly that gap. When the system can read your ad's text and imagery and relate it to ads it already understands, a genuinely new concept can be matched to the right people from its first impressions rather than after a costly warm-up. The practical flip side is that lazy creative now fails faster too: an ad that says nothing distinct has no tokens worth matching. Launch discipline follows from both halves. Ship new concepts in small batches inside a stable ad set, give each enough budget to be read, and judge them on early site-side signals rather than killing anything that does not spike in 48 hours.

What this means for a small business

None of this requires understanding GPU architectures, and none of it can be toggled in Ads Manager. It changes where your effort goes. The hours that used to go into building elaborate interest stacks are better spent writing three more angles of your offer and briefing one more creator. The review question changes too: not which interest performed, but which concept the system chose to spend behind, and whether you have given it a genuinely different next concept to test. This is also how we structure campaigns in our media buying work at NOVA.innovation: consolidated ad sets, a deliberate concept map instead of a pile of near-copies, and creative reviews that count angles rather than assets. The machine got smarter at matching; the human job is to give it real variety to match with.
