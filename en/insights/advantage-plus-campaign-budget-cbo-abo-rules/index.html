Advantage+ Campaign Budget vs Ad Set Budgets: The Documented Rules

Meta renamed CBO to Advantage+ campaign budget and switched it on by default. The documented rules: the 2-hour lock, the 70 ad set recommendation, best-effort spend limits, the 20 percent budget sharing option, and when each mode fits.

Every Meta campaign answers the same question twice: how much to spend, and who decides where it goes. The setting that answers the second question is the one media buyers still call CBO versus ABO, even though Meta retired those names. Meta's Business Help Center now calls the campaign-level option Advantage+ campaign budget, also shown as campaign budget or budget with Advantage+ on, and Sales, Leads and App promotion campaigns have it switched on by default. This article collects the documented mechanics: what the three budget options are, the rules Meta publishes about them, and where each one fits. Practitioner interpretation is labeled as such.

The three budget options Meta offers today

In Ads Manager you set the budget at one of two levels, the campaign or the ad set, and Meta's current documentation describes three arrangements. Advantage+ campaign budget, the old CBO: one budget at the campaign level, which the system distributes across ad sets in real time toward the best opportunities. Ad set budgets, the old ABO: each ad set carries its own daily or lifetime budget and spends it independently. And ad set budget sharing, a middle option on ABO campaigns with daily budgets: you keep a budget per ad set, but Meta may move up to 20 percent of one ad set's daily budget to other ad sets in the campaign when it sees better opportunities there.

Meta's developer documentation frames these as degrees of automation: campaign budget is full automation, because 100 percent of the budget is flexible, while budget sharing is partial automation. Ad set budgets without sharing are the only arrangement where the split stays entirely in your hands.

The naming problem

If you search Ads Manager for CBO you will not find it. Meta's help pages use Advantage+ campaign budget, the campaign setup screen may show campaign budget or budget with Advantage+ on, and some Marketing API documents write Advantage campaign budget without the plus. All of them name the same feature: one campaign budget the system allocates across ad sets. ABO, the paired term, stands for ad set budget optimization in buyer slang; Meta simply calls those ad set budgets. The rebrand matters practically because tutorials, agency playbooks and forum advice still speak CBO and ABO, while the interface speaks Advantage+. Knowing they are the same switch is the difference between following a guide and getting lost in the menus.

Why spend piles into one ad set

The behavior that alarms advertisers new to campaign budget, one ad set absorbing most of the money while others starve, is the feature working as documented. The system continuously reallocates toward whatever is producing the cheapest results right now. In a test where ad sets represent different hypotheses, that is exactly what you do not want: the test ends before the starved ad sets gather enough results to read. In a scaling setup where the ad sets are interchangeable audiences for the same offer, concentration is the point: the budget follows performance faster than any human rebalancing schedule.

How to read results under campaign budget

Meta tells advertisers to judge Advantage+ campaign budget at the campaign level, because per-ad-set costs swing as the system reallocates. That advice is half of the story. The documented mechanics mean a strong campaign average can hide an ad set spending heavily at a weak cost per result on a day the system guessed wrong, and a weak average can hide one ad set quietly carrying the account. Agencies that audit these campaigns read both layers: the campaign average for the true cost of the strategy, and per-ad-set spend and results over several days to see where the money actually went. Judging a single day under campaign budget is judging a reallocating system at one random moment.

Budget sharing, the quiet middle option

Budget sharing deserves attention because it changes what an ABO campaign means. With sharing on, your per-ad-set budgets are plans, not fences: Meta may move up to 20 percent of each ad set's daily budget elsewhere in the campaign. For accounts that chose ABO specifically to enforce a hard split, for example a fixed testing budget per audience, sharing quietly reintroduces the flexibility they were avoiding. Check whether it is enabled before assuming your ad set budgets are ceilings. For accounts that chose ABO for readability, sharing is usually harmless: the split stays mostly yours, and Meta trims the edges.

Daily versus lifetime budgets

Both levels offer daily and lifetime budgets, and the documented behavior differs in a way that matters. A daily budget is an average Meta can exceed on strong days and undershoot on weak ones, within a weekly cap, because the system paces spend toward opportunity rather than dividing it into equal slices. A lifetime budget commits the whole amount across a schedule and gives Meta more room to shift spend between days, which suits fixed-window pushes like a launch week or Ramadan campaign, and it unlocks dayparting on some setups. The learning phase math from earlier in this series applies either way: whatever level and mode you pick, each ad set still needs roughly 50 optimization events a week to deliver stably, so a budget too thin to fund that volume produces permanently unstable delivery no matter how it is allocated.

How we read this as operators

This section is our interpretation, not Meta's documentation. The documented mechanics support the pattern practitioners converge on. Use ad set budgets without sharing when you are testing: each hypothesis needs its own guaranteed spend, or the test data is unreadable. Move to Advantage+ campaign budget when you scale: the ad sets are interchangeable, volume matters more than per-ad-set control, and the system's reallocation speed becomes an advantage rather than interference. Keep ad set count lean under campaign budget; Meta's own 70-ad-set recommendation exists because sprawl lengthens the learning phase we covered earlier in this series. And treat min and max spend limits as emergency brakes, not steering wheels, exactly as Meta advises.

The second practical reading: name your choice. Because the same campaign can be Advantage+ at the budget level, manual at the audience level and automated again at the placement level, half of all budget confusion is teams not knowing which dials are on. A one-line note per campaign, budget: Advantage+ on, sharing: off, is documentation you will thank yourself for when you audit the account in three months.

What this means for a small business in the Gulf

For a small store in Saudi Arabia or Egypt with a modest daily budget, the decision is simpler than the options suggest. While you are testing, one campaign with two or three ad sets on separate ad set budgets, sharing off, gives every idea its fair spend and readable results. When one setup proves itself, consolidating into a single campaign budget lets the system push money to what works without daily manual rebalancing. And the two-hour lock matters more at small budgets: a toggle you regret cannot be undone before tomorrow's spend. Connect this to the learning phase math from earlier in the series, roughly 50 results per ad set per week, and the practical ceiling on ad set count sets itself: at small budgets, two funded ad sets beat five starved ones under any budget mode.

The deeper pattern matches the rest of this series. Just as Advantage+ made automation the default at the campaign level, Advantage+ campaign budget makes automation the default at the money level, and budget sharing extends it into campaigns that look manual. The advertiser's job in 2026 is not to fight the defaults or surrender to them, but to know exactly which are on, why, and what each one does to the evidence you collect. An account you can read beats an account you merely run.
