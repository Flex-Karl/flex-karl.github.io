Audience Research Before Ad Spend: First-Party Data, Personas, and the Questions That Matter

65 percent of marketers rarely or never research their audience. The four research layers, personas that must change a decision, and JTBD interviews, from five practitioner sources.

Here is the uncomfortable opening fact from the research literature: according to a large CoSchedule survey cited by Why We Buy (September 22, 2023), 65 percent of marketers report doing audience research rarely or never, and a CX Network study cited alongside it found 63 percent of consumers believe marketers are trying to sell them things they do not need. Two thirds of the industry is guessing, and two thirds of the audience can tell. David Ogilvy's line, quoted in the same piece, has aged well: advertising people who ignore research are as dangerous as generals who ignore decoded enemy signals.

Yet every targeting decision, every creative brief, every budget line in a media plan is an implicit claim about an audience: who they are, what they want, what they will believe. This article is about making those claims explicit and evidence-based before you spend, using five sources: Media Automation's practical guide to audience research for programmatic advertising (September 11, 2026), SOMIN's method for building market personas from audience data and competitor ads (September 30, 2026), AdConversion's application of the Jobs to Be Done framework to B2B advertising (October 23, 2026), Why We Buy's catalog of 23 customer research methods (September 2023), and AUDIENCES' guide to first-party data activation (November 5, 2026). Vendor case results are labeled as their accounts; our interpretation is marked.

Start with the decision, not the data

Media Automation's first rule reverses how most research projects begin. Someone asks for a new data source before anyone has named the decision it should inform. Instead, write one sentence describing the choice you face: whether to shift a fifth of display budget from broad prospecting to a lookalike built on recent purchasers, for example. That sentence sets your sample, your questions, and your success measure. It also tells you when to stop: a study that answers the decision is finished, and a study that merely describes a market is not. Their second rule is ownership: one analyst, one decision. Committees produce long decks and short action lists, and if two people cannot agree on the decision, the brief is not ready.

Their test of value is brutal and correct: research only earns its keep when it changes a targeting rule, a creative route, or a budget line. If no decision moves, the study was decoration. Keep that test in mind, because SOMIN applies the same test to personas, and it is the thread that holds this whole article together.

The four research layers, in order

Media Automation structures audience research as four layers that answer different questions, each with its own cost, bias, and place in the planning cycle:

Run the layers in that order, because each one narrows the next, and each is cheaper than guessing. Their matching rule is the practical takeaway: a category question needs a panel, a message question needs interviews, and a delivery question needs your own logs. Teams that buy a panel to answer a message question, or run interviews to size a market, spend research budget answering the wrong layer's question.

Build the first-party foundation first

The cheapest audience research is the data you already hold. Media Automation's foundation checklist: pull twelve months of site behavior, add purchase records and support contacts, segment by recency, frequency, and value, and look for clusters that behave alike rather than clusters that look tidy in a slide. Record the share of sessions you can actually identify, by channel and by device, because the gap between a low match rate on one source and a high rate on another decides which audiences you can build without bought data.

Two warnings come with this layer. First, consent: their guide is written under UK GDPR, where purpose matters and data collected to fulfill an order is not automatically available for advertising, so record the lawful basis for each use and keep suppression lists current; whatever your jurisdiction, the discipline of checking consent coverage before building on data is the same. Second, expect gaps: most advertisers find their first-party data over-represents loyal buyers and under-represents everyone else. That bias is not a failure of your data; it is a property of it, and it is exactly the point where bought research earns its budget.

The commercial case for taking this layer seriously comes from the activation side. AUDIENCES' 2026 guide (November 5, 2026) argues first-party customer data is among a marketer's most valuable assets and reports their own customers achieving, on average, a 33 percent reduction in cost per acquisition and a 22 percent increase in return on ad spend through in-cloud activation. That is the vendor's average across their own customer base, not an industry benchmark, and we cite it as their account. But it indicates the order of value practitioners claim for actually using what you already hold, which is relevant context when research feels like a cost center.

Size the market with official statistics before buying fieldwork

Media Automation's fourth layer is the one teams skip because it feels academic, and their argument for it is practical. Before commissioning fieldwork, check what already exists: in the UK, the Office for National Statistics publishes annual figures on internet access and device ownership, which gives you a defensible base for reach assumptions rather than a vendor's estimate. Read the release notes, not just the tables, because question wording and coverage change between years and a like-for-like comparison across releases can be an illusion. Then use the figures to sanity-check the reach models your platform or demand-side partner shows you: if a planner claims ninety percent coverage of a demographic that official data suggests is less connected, ask to see the working. Official data will not tell you which message works, but it will stop you building a plan on a false picture of who is online and how. Every market has an equivalent statistical authority; the discipline transfers even where the specific source does not.

Personas that earn their place

SOMIN's method (September 30, 2026) builds market personas one market at a time from three inputs: panel or survey data on who buys, competitor ads showing who rivals already target and with what promise, and public conversation revealing the tensions people voice. Cluster each market separately, check the clusters against the ads that actually perform there, and keep only the personas that change a message, an offer, or a channel. Their takeaways include the same value test as Media Automation, sharpened: a market persona earns its place only if it changes what you say, offer, or spend in that market. A demographic label like Gen Z is a starting filter, not a persona; their whitespace study of 229 audience-research posts found a third of them grouped under one tension, brands mistargeting by leaning too heavily on generational labels.

Their case examples, told as SOMIN's own client work and cited here as the vendor's account: for Miraflora Wagyu, a premium beef brand with a high acquisition cost and a suspected price problem, GWI panel data among affluent Colorado cooks showed 61.4 percent use discount codes, 61.0 percent look for the best deals, and 67.0 percent use loyalty programs, reframing the problem from price sensitivity to deal sensitivity, which is a different creative and offer strategy entirely. For Keeta in Hong Kong, the same method removed price language from every direct-to-action line because the target audience under-indexed on budget behavior. Note also their process discipline: fix the market and qualifying behavior first (their Colorado example started from 2.8 million cooking-interested people and narrowed to 1.3 million above a household income threshold), pull competitor ads for that market, cluster, validate against audience data, and write each persona as a set of decisions. Personas drift month to month, so rebuild on a schedule and keep each one's sample size visible.

For teams with research budget, SOMIN points to what Nielsen Norman Group calls statistical personas: qualitative work first, then a survey of at least 100 people, ideally 500 or more, and a clustering method. NN/g notes the approach is expensive and needs statistical skill. The lighter version for most accounts: qualitative interviews to surface the tensions, your own customer data to size them, and competitor ads to see which tensions the category already fights over.

The qualitative layer: jobs to be done interviews

Numbers tell you what clusters exist; only conversations tell you why. AdConversion's guide (October 23, 2026) applies Tony Ulwick's Jobs to Be Done framework to advertising: people do not buy products, they hire solutions to get a job done, and identifying the job your customers are hiring you for is the foundation of the campaign's message. Their starting move is deliberately small: identify three to five recent customers who fit your ideal customer profile, and interview them about why they chose you and which pain points you solved. The output is not a transcript archive; it is a matrix of jobs, pains, and desired outcomes that maps directly to ad angles.

Why We Buy's catalog of 23 methods (September 2023) widens the qualitative toolkit well beyond customer interviews, and its most practical entries are the ones already inside your building. Interview the people who work directly with the audience: in an agency that means your client; in-house it means colleagues in sales and customer success, who can already recite the patterns they hear but may not flag the ones a marketer needs, which is why listening to sales calls yourself, or taking a turn on the support line, unearths what internal summaries miss. Mine what customers already produce unprompted: reviews, support tickets, community posts, and comment threads, while accepting the limits of observation, namely that insights stay surface-level, you cannot ask follow-ups, and you learn only what people organically volunteer. Their survey guidance carries a useful humility: surveying is part art and part science, ask too many questions and participants abandon, ask the right questions the wrong way and you get misleading answers. The principle across both sources matches Media Automation's: qualitative work is the layer that gives you language, motivation, and objections, the raw material of hooks and copy. An audience research program that ends in a segment spreadsheet but never captures the words customers use has skipped the layer advertising most needs.

What this means for your operation

Assembled, the practitioner consensus is a sequence. Name the decision first. Answer it with the cheapest layer that can: your own behavioral and CRM data for who already converts and where the journey breaks, panels for sizing and category context, interviews and internal observation for language and motivation, official statistics for market framing. Turn the findings into personas only where a persona changes a message, an offer, or a channel, and rebuild them on a schedule because audiences drift. And run the whole thing as a loop, not a project: each campaign you launch generates the behavioral data that sharpens the next round of research.

In our work managing paid campaigns, audience research is the step we refuse to skip, because every weakness it would have caught surfaces later anyway, as creative that fails to resonate, targeting that guesses, and reports that cannot explain themselves. That is our operating position, an interpretation of the sources above rather than their claim. If your last persona came from a brainstorming session rather than from data and conversations, rebuilding it is one of the highest-leverage pieces of groundwork available, and it is work we can do with you.
